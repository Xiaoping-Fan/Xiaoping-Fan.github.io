Research

Understanding how plant–microbe interactions and microbial functional guilds determine whether nitrogen is retained within soil–plant systems or lost to the environment, and how these controls persist under environmental change.

Overview

My research asks how plant–microbe interactions regulate the fate of nitrogen in agroecosystems, using microbial nitrogen transformations to understand how nitrogen is partitioned between losses to the environment and plant acquisition.

I am particularly interested in how microbial functional guilds respond to plant traits, environmental conditions and ecological history. As climate warming intensifies, understanding whether these responses can sustain nitrogen retention, plant nutrition and the resilience of soil–plant systems is increasingly important.

01

Microbial guilds → nitrogen fate

Microbial controls on nitrogen retention and loss

Nitrogen-transforming microorganisms determine how rapidly nitrogen moves through soils and which pathways dominate. My work investigates the functional ecology of nitrifiers, denitrifiers and N₂O-reducing microorganisms, with particular emphasis on ecological differentiation among ammonia-oxidising archaea and bacteria and the contribution of comammox Nitrospira.

I am interested in how soil properties, substrate availability, fertilisation and nitrification inhibitors alter the abundance and activity of these guilds, and how such changes influence nitrogen retention within the soil–plant system and gaseous nitrogen losses, including N₂O emissions.

02

Plants → rhizosphere nitrogen

Plant regulation of rhizosphere nitrogen availability

Plants are active regulators of rhizosphere biogeochemistry. I investigate how genotype, developmental stage and root-derived compounds alter nitrification and the ecology of nitrogen-transforming microorganisms, thereby changing the forms and retention of nitrogen available within the rhizosphere.

A major focus is biological nitrification inhibition (BNI) in cereals and the role of root metabolites in mediating this process. This work asks when plant-mediated control of nitrification is expressed, which microbial guilds respond, and whether variation among plant genotypes can be used to improve nitrogen acquisition while reducing nitrogen losses.

03

Environmental change → ecosystem function

Environmental drivers on microbial resilience and ecosystem function

Soil microbial communities are continuously shaped by temperature, resource availability, agricultural management and their own ecological history. My work examines how these drivers reorganise microbial communities and food webs and how those changes propagate to biogeochemical processes and plant performance.

Building on this work, I am increasingly interested in resistance, recovery and ecological legacy under repeated disturbance. In a warming climate, a central question is whether past environmental conditions alter future microbial responses and whether those history-dependent responses enhance or erode the capacity of soil–plant systems to retain nitrogen and maintain function.

Approaches

From microbial mechanisms to field‑scale ecosystem function

I work across experimental scales to connect direct microbial responses with soil nitrogen processes, plant–rhizosphere interactions and outcomes under realistic agricultural conditions.
